The Harbour Ledger affair was the discovery that a criminal network had been laundering money through the freight trade at the old harbour for most of a decade. It is named after the forwarding company at
its centre, and it led directly to the founding of the [[security-bureau]].

## The scheme

Harbour Ledger Forwarding booked cargo that did not exist. Its invoices, paid by companies registered in the free port,
moved money from abroad into District bank accounts as the proceeds of trade, and from there into property on the
waterfront and shares on the Aerie Exchange. At its peak the company invoiced more freight than the old harbour could
physically handle in a year.

## How it came out

It came out from the mainland. A prosecutor there, following a narcotics case, asked the District for the bank records
behind three of Harbour Ledger's customers. The request went to the Trade & Migration Office, which handled the
borders; to the municipal police, who handled the streets; and to the Credit Registry, which kept the records but had
no power to release them. None of them could act on it, and for eleven months none did.

When the mainland published its case, the District learned of the scheme from the newspapers. Harbour Ledger's owners
had left by then, and most of the money with them.

## What followed

The Council treated the affair as a threat to the District's standing as a financial centre, which rests on the
mainland's trust that District money is clean. Within the year it founded the Security Bureau by decree, and the
Bureau's first case was the remains of Harbour Ledger: forty-one companies in the free port, closed, and the
property bought with their money, seized.
