## The House

On Glasswater Row, Peregrine is called the House, as in the house always collects its edge. When a retail trader buys a
call option or a fund buys protection against a fall, Peregrine is usually the counterparty, and it takes the spread
whichever way the market then goes. One of its founding partners put the firm's view of its customers in a speech it has
since asked to have left out of the record: "Bulls and bears bleed exactly the same. We simply own the slaughterhouse."

## New money

Peregrine is not a founding house, and it has never wanted to be mistaken for one. It was started a generation after the
Charter by a group of mathematicians and engineers who had left the founding houses' trading floors, convinced that the
houses were too slow to price the risks they were taking. It began by quoting options on the Exchange's largest
companies when few others would, and found that buyers of options pay more for them, on average, than the options turn
out to be worth.

Its partners were among the younger members who broke with the Vanguard over the founding houses' privileges, and the
firm helped pay for New Horizon's first campaign, against a rise in the stamp duty on share trades. The rise was
reversed. Peregrine, which trades more often than anyone else in the District, has never claimed to be disinterested.

## How it earns

About 60% of revenue comes from writing options: premium paid by retail traders buying calls and by funds buying
protection against a fall, which Peregrine keeps when the market turns out calmer than the price it charged. The other
40% comes from trading, the bid-ask spread on the orders it fills and the retail order flow it internalises.

The firm takes no view on direction. It hedges continuously to keep its book close to delta-neutral, on its own trading
hardware and a private microwave network. The operating margin is about 35%, and it pays out about 40% of earnings.

## The hedge that pays twice

Peregrine's shares have a beta of about 0.9, low for a trading house, because what hurts everyone else lifts its
revenue. Calm bull markets bring modest, steady results. Sell-offs lift volumes, widen spreads and raise the price of
protection, and the firm has reported its best quarters during market stress. Some fund managers hold the shares as a
partial hedge, which means they are paying Peregrine for protection twice.

Much of its retail flow comes from Finch Securities, the District's largest retail broker, which sends Peregrine its
clients' orders and is paid for each one. Finch's clients trade without commission and Peregrine fills them against its
own quotes; both firms describe the arrangement as good for the small investor. Peregrine is also one of the few large
desks in the District that does not use a Tickbird terminal, which this desk records without comment.

## What the desk is watching

A firm that sells insurance against large moves earns a little on most days and pays out on the day the move arrives.
Peregrine's losses come when prices gap faster than its hedges can be adjusted: an overnight jump, a halted stock, a
market that opens far below where it closed. The question for shareholders is whether the premiums of the calm years
cover that day. So far they have, and the firm's partners point out that this is what the people who sold them their
first options thought too.
