## The water, not the ships

The asset managers on Glasswater Row trade on the surface of the District's markets. Lakebird Bank is most of the water
underneath. It holds most of the District's deposits, runs its largest employers' payrolls, writes most of its mortgages
and leads most of the loans syndicated on the Row.

Visitors often take it for the central bank. It is not. The Monetary Authority sets the policy rate and the Financial
Regulator sets Lakebird's capital requirement, and both have said so in writing more than once. The confusion is
understandable: for most households and companies, the Authority's rate arrives in a letter from Lakebird.

## Born in a merger

The District began as a proposal nobody wanted. The mainland was deep in a bust that showed no sign of ending: banks
were failing faster than they could be wound up, and the funds that might have bought their assets were short of money
themselves. Leonard Ashcombe, an economist in the mainland's finance department, proposed a self-governing enclave on
the coast with light rules and low taxes, where capital would return because it was allowed to. Government and private
industry both turned him down.

The proposal was about to be shelved when the Breakwater family put their name to it. Their Breakwater Trust already
owned the foundries and deepwater berths on [[district-territory|the peninsula]], and with the family behind it a
coalition of banks with nothing left to lose signed on. Their condition for entry was that they merge. Lakebird is the
result: more than a dozen lenders combined into one, bringing their deposits, their branches and their bad loans with
them.

The shares issued in the merger went into founders' trusts that the Charter barred from selling for a generation, and
most were kept after the bar lapsed. About half the bank is still held that way and almost never changes hands. Ashcombe
was given no stake and asked for none. His portrait hangs in the Lakebird boardroom, one floor below the founders'.

## The Syndicate

The bad loans became a habit. Writing them off would have written off a good part of the District's industry, so when
industrial and logistics borrowers could not repay, Lakebird swapped the debt for equity and kept the stakes. The first
swaps carried cash sweeps and first liens; most are ordinary shares now, and what reaches the bank is dividends, a
steady fifth of its revenue. The companies are known as the Lakebird Syndicate. On Glasswater Row it is said that a
Syndicate board meets twice, once in its own offices and once on the fourteenth floor at Lakebird.

The habit runs through its other relationships. Lakebird and Black Swan Capital together own a majority of Iron Beak,
the contractor on most of the civic projects the bank finances, and of Condor Extraction, several of whose foreign
mining concessions came from governments that defaulted on Lakebird loans and surrendered the land they had pledged. The
bank also sponsored Plaza Civic River Trust, which carries much of the municipal borrowing it arranges. Few analysts
treat the trust as independent, and the trust has not asked them to.

## How it earns

Most of the profit is the spread between what Lakebird pays depositors and what it earns on loans and securities.
Residential mortgages make up close to a third of the loan book; the rest is consumer credit, commercial property and
the syndicated loans it leads. Fees from payments, payroll and the licence for the Lakebird 30 index add a further fifth
of revenue.

By District standards the bank is run conservatively. It holds capital well above the Financial Regulator's requirement,
pays out about 40% of earnings and keeps the rest for the next downturn. Plover Savings Bank is its only real competitor
for mortgages, and Brine Pool Capital lends where Lakebird is held back by the Regulator's capital rules.

## What the desk is watching

Clients sometimes ask the desk for a way to hold the District as a whole. The honest answer is LAKE. Its loans reach
most of the economy, so its earnings grow with it, widen with a steeper yield curve and narrow when the curve flattens.
Its losses arrive at the same time as everyone else's: the shares have fallen hardest when rates rose quickly into a
slowdown, flattening the curve and lifting defaults together.

Its commercial paper is among the most widely held short-term assets on the Aerie Exchange. Some desks keep the paper in
place of Exchequer bills, on the reasoning that if Lakebird ever failed to pay, the bills would not be worth much
either. The desk does not share the reasoning and has not managed to fault it.

The bank has never needed outside capital. Shareholders say this as reassurance. Regulators have been heard to say it as
a worry, since nobody in the District is large enough to supply it if it ever did.
