## Flags

- **[P]**: I read the number in the primary source text or table myself (page, table or figure given).
- **[P-fig]**: I read the value off a primary-source figure by eye. It is approximate (about ±5% of the axis span), and no table in the source prints it.
- **[D]**: I computed it from the author's own posted replication dataset. The method is stated. The number is not printed in the paper.
- **[S]**: I saw it only secondhand: in an abstract, a summary, or another paper's citation.
- **NOT FOUND**: I could not obtain the number. I did not guess a value.

## 1. Binder, C. (2021) "Political Pressure on Central Banks", JMCB 53(4): 715–744

**Access.** The article is closed access. Wiley returned 403 (Cloudflare), SSRN 3244148 returned 403, the Haverford Scholarship page (economics_facpubs/225) has metadata only, and OpenAlex/Unpaywall report `oa_status: closed`. The full paper was not read, so **all regression coefficients, marginal effects and inflation-effect estimates from the JMCB article are NOT FOUND.**

I used three of Binder's own substitutes instead.

### 1a. Abstract (RePEc ideas page) [S]

- Quarterly dataset, 118 central banks, 2010–2018.
- About 10% of central banks face political pressure in an average year.
- Banks with legal independence still face pressure, which is nearly always for looser policy.
- Pressure is associated with higher inflation and higher inflation persistence.
- Pressure is more likely under left-wing or nationalist executives, few checks and balances, and weak electoral competition.

### 1b. Conference slides, SUERF/BAFFI CAREFIN conference, 8 Nov 2019 [P]

Source: `suerf.org/.../l_f2fc...24045_suerf-1.pdf`.

- **Slide 6, method.** Text search of EIU (2010Q1–2018Q2) and BMI (2008Q1–2018Q2) country reports; over 500 relevant reports; each coded resist or succumb.
- **Slides 7–8, coding.**
  - *Resist (Category 1)*: the report notes the bank is resisting, or possibly resisting, actual, potential or perceived pressure.
  - *Succumb (Category 2)*: pressure affects or will affect policy, or the government directly controls the bank or the money supply.
- **Slide 9, composition of coded reports.**
  - Pressure to ease **91%**; to tighten **3%**. The Fed is one of only 6 banks pressured to tighten (2011–12).
  - Replacement of the central banker discussed in **15%**.
  - Elections discussed in **26%**.
  - Central-bank legislation discussed in **4%**.
- **Slide 10, sample.** 118 banks covered by both sources; 39 quarters; **4602** bank-quarter observations in the main analysis.
- **Slide 12.** Pressure is "nearly orthogonal to legal CBI (Garriga 2016)" and correlated with irregular governor turnover.
- **Slide 14.** Jordà local projections of CPI inflation on unemployment, IP growth and category-1 and category-2 dummies, with country fixed effects. The coefficients are only in a figure image, which did not survive text conversion: **NOT FOUND**.
- **Slide 15.** Inflation is more persistent where the bank faces pressure, "especially if the bank succumbs" (Dickey–Fuller tests by country, or panel AR). No numbers given.
- **Slide 16.** Pressure is more prevalent with less democracy and low electoral competitiveness, weaker checks and balances, and nationalist or populist leaders.

### 1c. Binder, C. (2020) "De facto and de jure central bank independence"

In Gnan & Masciandaro (eds.), *Populism, Economic Policies and Central Banking*, SUERF/Larcier, pp.129–136. Source: `suerf.org/.../doc_f2217062..._11095_suerf.pdf`. All [P].

- p.130:
  - Balanced panel, 118 banks, quarterly since 2010.
  - **39%** of banks face pressure at least once.
  - In an average quarter **6%** face reported pressure.
  - Peak **14% in 2019Q1**.
- p.130, by region (average quarter): Americas 5.2%, Europe 4.5%, Africa 6.9%, Asia-Pacific 4.5%.
- p.130–131, rankings.
  - Most frequent pressure: Angola, Turkey, Argentina, Myanmar, Venezuela, Syria, Japan.
  - Most frequent succumbing: Turkey, Myanmar, Venezuela, Angola, Argentina, Vietnam, BEAC.
- **p.131, ideology** (Polity IV and DPI data):
  - "Political pressure is less likely to come from an executive from a centrist party than from an executive from a right-leaning or left-leaning party."
  - Pressure is less frequent in highly democratic countries and with strong checks and balances.
  - "The strongest and perhaps most interesting predictor of political pressure on the central bank is a nationalist executive or nationalist ruling party."
  - Coefficients are not given in the chapter: **NOT FOUND**.
- **p.131, Fig. 2, populism.** Global Populism Database (Hawkins et al. 2019); 32 banks, 19 with a somewhat-populist-or-more leader; 2010Q1–2019Q1.
  - Text: pressure is "about twice as prevalent for the populist group".
  - Bars [P-fig]: ≈0.31 (not populist) vs ≈0.68 (populist), measured as the share of banks ever facing pressure. These heights equal 4/13 and 13/19; that equality is my inference, not stated.
- **p.132, legal CBI.** Legal CBI (Garriga 2016) is "virtually orthogonal to frequency of political pressure". Turkey: legal CBI 0.9 (around the 90th percentile) with pressure in **60%** of quarters (Fig. 3 scatter).
- **p.132, resisting still costs inflation.** "even when the central bank reportedly resists the political pressure, inflation still tends to rise following an episode of pressure." The proposed mechanism is credibility and expectations. No magnitude given.
- **p.133.** About **1.5%** of central banks have a legal CBI reduction each year in 2010–2012 (Garriga data). Legal reductions are much rarer than pressure.

## 2. Gavin, M. & Manger, M. (2023) "Populism and De Facto Central Bank Independence"

*Comparative Political Studies* 56(8). Read from the PMC / UToronto copy, online-first pagination 1–35. PMC HTML tables were used to recover minus signs. All [P].

**Sample and data.**
- EIU reports 1996–2016; 35 low- to upper-middle-income countries (Table 2).
- Excludes hard pegs, monetary unions and free-falls, and banks with Garriga legal CBI below 0.4.
- Populism is the V-Party 0–1 score; Hawkins et al. (2019) is used in robustness checks.
- Quarterly frequency; N = 2000.

**Pressure frequency and coding.**
- Public pressure to ease is "not uncommon, occurring in approximately four percent of country-months" in the 2100 hand-coded reports (p.14).
- A pressure event is coded only when the report says a government member publicly demanded lower rates. The paper does not use the analyst's resist/succumb judgement (pp.13–14).
- Classifier accuracy (Table 1, p.15): sensitivity 0.58, specificity 0.98.
- p.7: "with the exception of the United States and Japan, we do not observe public pressure on central banks in developed countries in our data."

**Table 3, probit (Carter–Signorino cubic time).**

| Coefficient | (2) | (3) | (5) preferred | (8) hand-coded only, N = 415 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Populism | 0.863*** (0.262) | 0.873*** | 0.724** (0.250) | 0.638*** (0.140) |
| Economic left–right (higher = right) | — | — | −0.263*** (0.071) | — |
| Populism × left–right | — | — | 0.470** (0.173) | — |
| Lagged capital outflows/GDP | — | — | −0.001* | — |
| Democracy (Polity) | −0.064 | — | −0.062 (n.s.) | — |
| <12 months to election | −0.231 (0.174) | — | −0.212 (0.168) | — |
| IMF CBI condition | — | — | −0.541 (n.s.) | — |

- Debt/GDP, lagged inflation, Δexchange rate and ΔGDP are all not significant.
- Pseudo-R² is 0.345 in model 5.
- The time-since-last-pressure term is significantly negative: "the longer the central bank independence has been respected… the less likely a future public attack" (p.22).

**Magnitudes and interpretation.**
- p.21: "the probability of pressure… is around **3.7 times greater** when we move from the lowest to the highest populism score in the sample." This is an average marginal effect.
- p.21–22: "sufficiently populist right-wing parties are most likely to attack… very economically left-wing parties are only likely to do so when they are not very populist". The authors add that this "could be a type-1 error"; pairwise Šidák-adjusted tests are significant only at extremes.
- p.22: capital outflows from mean to +1 sd reduce pressure probability "by around 45 percent".

**Table 4, multinomial probit, p.23.**
- Pressure: populism 1.067** (0.345).
- Irregular governor turnover: populism 0.306 (0.332), not significant; lagged ΔGDP −6.949**.
- Weakening of legal CBI: populism 0.687 (0.715), not significant.
- Conclusion (p.27): populists are "no more or less likely to remove central bank governors or change central bank laws than other governments."

**Effects, local projections (Ramey–Zubairy state-dependent), p.25–26.**
- States are split at the upper quartile of populism. Examples (p.26):
  - Low-populist: Netanyahu 2015–19 = 0.647; N. Kirchner = 0.659.
  - High-populist: Fernández de Kirchner = 0.869; Erdoğan = 0.962.
- "For a single pressure event, high-populism governments obtain a reduction of the monetary policy rate of about **0.3 percentage points** on average after three quarters."
- Low-populist: the 95% CI always includes 0.
- Inflation: "a high-populist pressure shock causes inflation to increase **0.5 percentage points** faster after six quarters", significant at 5% after three quarters (Fig. 7).
- Coefficient table A-3.5 is in the online appendix and was not retrieved: **NOT FOUND**.

**Model parameters.** The formal model's parameter values (fn 10: α = 0.15, r = 0.1, γ = 0.25, π = 0.3) are illustrative assumptions, not estimates. Do not use them as calibration.

**Binder (2021) secondhand, via Gavin & Manger p.5–6 [S].**
- "Binder finds that left and nationalist parties are more likely to pressure."
- Binder's "nationalist" parties "are not right-wing parties of European populism, but parties like the Algerian National Liberation Front".

## 4. Funke, Schularick & Trebesch, "Populist Leaders and the Economy"

The AER 2023 final version was not readable (HAL and EconStor bot walls). Read: ECONtribute DP 036, Oct 2020 (190 pp.). Numbers may differ from the AER version.

**No central bank variables.** The paper does **not** measure central bank independence or governor turnover. "central bank" appears only in references. Its institutional outcomes are V-Dem judicial constraints, clean elections and press freedom.

**Populist leaders and tenure.**
- p.12–13: 50 populist leaders out of 1,458 (3.4%) and 72 spells out of 1,827 (3.9%), 1900–2018, 60 countries. 35 right-wing and 37 left-wing spells. [P]
- p.16: average populist career in office >8 years vs 4 years for non-populists; 34% return to office (16% for non-populists). [P]

**Inflation, Fig. 15 p.40 (synthetic-control "doppelganger gap", pp of inflation)** [P-fig]:
- All populists: ≈+15 pp around year 4.
- Left-wing: ≈+25 pp around year 4 and ≈+27 pp at year 13.
- Right-wing: ≈+7 pp at year 4, near 0 otherwise.
- Text: "Inflation rises meaningfully under left-wing populists… Cases of right-wing populist rule that resulted in high inflation are rare… [except] Erdoğan." [P]

**Institutions, p.41.** Institutional indices are 5–15 index points below counterfactual after 10 years. [P]

## 6. Drechsel, T. (2024) "Estimating the Effects of Political Pressure on the Fed", NBER WP 32461

Read from `drechsel.txt`, with figures rendered from the NBER PDF.

**Data (p.1–2, 7–9) [P].**
- 1933–2016 presidential daily schedules; >800 interactions.
- Mean length 53 minutes; 36% one-on-one; 11% on weekends; 16% social settings; 92% with the Chair.
- Nixon 160 interactions, Clinton 6.
- Nixon met Burns 17 times in 1971Q3 and 17 times in 1971Q4 (p.24); 34 interactions over 6 months (fn 20).

**Specification (p.19–21) [P].**
- Quarterly SVAR, 1933Q1–2016Q4, 8 lags.
- Variables: interactions, log GDP deflator, 3-month Tbill (Wu–Xia shadow rate at the zero lower bound), log government spending, log real GDP, deficit/GDP.
- Sign restrictions: interactions +, deflator + (on impact), Tbill − (dropped in the "lighter" variant).
- Narrative "overwhelming" restriction on 1971Q3–Q4.

**Shock normalisation (p.24) [P].**
- +10 interactions in one quarter.
- "The average number of quarterly interactions is 3, with a standard deviation of 2.7."

**Impulse responses, Fig. 7 p.25** (median, 68% credible band):

## 9. Resistance: what the evidence says

1. **Share that resist.**
   - Binder's coding: about 60% of pressure quarters are "resist" and 40% "succumb" [D].
   - Advanced economies succumb less: 22% vs 42% [D].
   - Succumbing is stickier: 60% of succumb quarters are followed by another succumb quarter, vs 46% resist→resist [D].
2. **Legal independence does not prevent pressure.** Legal CBI is "virtually orthogonal" to pressure frequency (Binder 2020 p.132 [P]; slides 12 [P]).
3. **Resisting is not free.** "Even when the central bank reportedly resists… inflation still tends to rise" (Binder 2020 p.132 [P]). No magnitude: **NOT FOUND**.
   - Drechsel's variant without the rate restriction (allows no rate cut) gives a *larger* price response (Fig. 8; text 7% vs 5%) [P]. This is consistent with an expectations channel that works even when the rate is held.
4. **Who wins.**
   - In emerging markets, high-populist governments get a ≈0.3 pp cut after 3 quarters; low-populist governments get no significant cut (Gavin & Manger p.26 [P]).
   - Financial-market discipline (capital outflows +1 sd) lowers pressure probability by about 45% (p.22 [P]).
5. **Individual central bankers.**
   - Burns (a partisan friend of Nixon) gave in; Martin under LBJ in 1965 reportedly did not (Drechsel p.12, p.39 [P]). This is narrative only.
   - **No study found that interacts the governor's hawkishness or legal CBI with the pressure effect: NOT FOUND.**
6. **Removal is not the main channel.** Populism does not raise irregular governor turnover (Gavin & Manger Table 4; Scheurer et al. Table 2 [P]).

## 10. Timing: what the evidence says

- **Elections.**
  - 26% (slides) to 33% ([D]) of Binder's pressure reports discuss elections.
  - Gavin & Manger's "<12 months to election" coefficient is negative and not significant (−0.21 to −0.33). The authors "do not observe a political business cycle effect" (p.27 [P]).
  - Governor replacement *is* more likely in the 12 months after an election (Dreher et al. logit 0.650 [P]).
- **The Nixon case is the canonical pre-election episode.**
  - Pressure peaked 1971Q3–Q4, 4–5 quarters before the November 1972 election.
  - Nixon told Burns he could tighten after April 1972.
  - Fed funds fell ~4.7 pp (Jan 1970 → Jan 1972), then rose ~5.2 pp in the year after the election (Abrams Table 1, p.25 [P]).
- **Trump.** He turned from hawkish critic (2011–13) to dovish pressure as incumbent (2018–20) (Bianchi et al. App. p.12 [P]).
- **Lags.**
  - Drechsel: expectations and media coverage respond only after about 3 years (p.38 [P]).
  - Gavin & Manger: rate cut after about 3 quarters, inflation after about 6 quarters [P].
